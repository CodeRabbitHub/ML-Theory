# Loss Functions — Senior ML Engineer Interview Prep (2026)

> Ties together nearly every earlier topic (linear/logistic regression's loss choices, gradient boosting's pluggable-loss framework, focal loss for imbalance) — senior interviews use this as an integrative topic to check you understand *why* a given loss is the right surrogate for a given business objective.

## TL;DR Refresher

- **Regression**: MSE/L2 (penalizes large errors quadratically — sensitive to outliers), MAE/L1 (penalizes linearly — robust to outliers, but non-differentiable at 0), Huber loss (quadratic for small errors, linear for large — a tunable compromise via threshold `δ`), Quantile/Pinball loss (asymmetric, targets a specific quantile rather than the mean — used for prediction intervals).
- **Classification**: Binary cross-entropy/log-loss (proper scoring rule, penalizes confident wrong predictions heavily), Hinge loss (SVM's margin-based loss, zero loss once correctly classified beyond the margin), Focal loss (down-weights easy examples — see the Imbalance doc), Categorical cross-entropy/softmax loss (multiclass generalization of log-loss).
- A loss function is a **surrogate** for the true (often non-differentiable) business/evaluation metric (e.g., 0/1 accuracy, ranking metrics) — the choice of surrogate encodes assumptions about error cost symmetry, outlier sensitivity, and what "wrong" means, and should be a deliberate engineering decision, not a framework default left unexamined.

## What's New / State of the Art (2025–2026)

- **Loss-function design as a direct lever for business alignment (not just model fit) is a bigger part of 2025–2026 senior interviews** than in earlier years — expect to be asked to *derive or choose* a loss that reflects a specific asymmetric business cost (as in the Imbalance and Evaluation Metrics docs' cost-matrix discussions), rather than just define standard losses from memory.
- **Focal loss's spread beyond computer vision into tabular/structured data** (fraud detection, structured imbalanced classification) continues through 2025, with adaptive/enhanced variants tuning the focusing parameter `γ` per-class or per-difficulty-stratum rather than using one global `γ` ([Adaptive focal loss with stratification, Scientific Reports 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-22853-y), [Batch-balanced focal loss](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10289178/)).
- **Contrastive and ranking losses (InfoNCE, triplet loss, cosine-embedding loss) are now baseline knowledge**, not a niche deep-learning topic — because embedding models (trained with these losses) underpin the RAG/vector-search/recommendation systems discussed throughout this repo's 2026 updates (KNN, Clustering, Distances docs), understanding *why* an embedding model trained with a contrastive loss produces a space where cosine similarity is meaningful is now expected senior-level knowledge, connecting loss-function choice directly to downstream retrieval behavior.
- **Differentiable surrogates for genuinely non-differentiable business metrics** (ranking metrics like NDCG, or F1 itself) remain an active, sophisticated topic — e.g., LambdaRank/LambdaMART-style losses that use a differentiable proxy for the gradient of a ranking metric — worth naming if the conversation moves toward recommendation/search-ranking systems.

## Senior-Level Interview Questions

**Q1. Derive why MSE gives a heavily outlier-sensitive model, and precisely how Huber loss fixes this while remaining differentiable.**
Strong answer: MSE's gradient with respect to the prediction is proportional to the error itself (`∂/∂ŷ [½(y-ŷ)²] = ŷ-y`), so a large error contributes a proportionally large gradient — outliers dominate the parameter updates. MAE's gradient is constant magnitude regardless of error size (robust to outliers) but is discontinuous/non-differentiable at zero error, causing optimization difficulties near convergence. Huber loss defines `L = ½(y-ŷ)²` for `|y-ŷ|≤δ` and `δ·(|y-ŷ|-½δ)` for `|y-ŷ|>δ` — quadratic (smooth, well-behaved gradient) near zero error, linear (bounded gradient magnitude, outlier-robust) for large errors, giving you both differentiability everywhere and bounded outlier influence, controlled by the single threshold `δ`.

**Q2. Why is log-loss (cross-entropy) the "correct" loss for a probabilistic classifier, in a way that, say, MSE on predicted probabilities is not?**
Strong answer: Log-loss is a **strictly proper scoring rule** — it's uniquely minimized in expectation when the predicted probability exactly matches the true underlying probability, which directly incentivizes calibrated probability estimates during training. MSE on probabilities is also technically a (less commonly used) proper scoring rule (the Brier score is MSE on probabilities), but log-loss's logarithmic penalty for confident-wrong predictions (`-log(ε)→∞` as predicted probability of the true class `→0`) more aggressively punishes overconfident mistakes, which is generally the more desirable training signal for classifiers, and it falls out naturally as the negative log-likelihood under a Bernoulli/categorical model — the maximum-likelihood-estimation justification, not an arbitrary engineering choice.

**Q3. Explain hinge loss and why an SVM trained with it produces a fundamentally different decision boundary than logistic regression trained with log-loss, even though both are "linear classifiers."**
Strong answer: Hinge loss `max(0, 1 - y·f(x))` gives **exactly zero** loss/gradient once a point is correctly classified beyond the margin (`y·f(x) ≥ 1`) — points far from the boundary on the correct side don't influence the solution at all (hence "support vectors" — only boundary-adjacent points matter). Log-loss, by contrast, never reaches exactly zero for any finite prediction and keeps pushing every point (even confidently, correctly classified ones) to become *more* confident, since `-log(σ(z))>0` for all finite `z`. This is why logistic regression's decision boundary is influenced by the whole dataset's distribution while SVM's is determined purely by the support vectors near the margin — a direct, derivable consequence of the loss function's shape, not an incidental algorithmic difference.

**Q4. Your team wants to fine-tune a text embedding model so that cosine similarity between embeddings reflects semantic similarity. What loss function would you use, and why does the loss choice determine what "similar" ends up meaning geometrically?**
Strong answer: A contrastive loss (InfoNCE / normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy, as used in SimCLR/CLIP-style training) — for each anchor, it pulls the embedding of a known-similar ("positive") example closer (higher cosine similarity / lower angular distance) while pushing embeddings of dissimilar ("negative") examples apart, directly in terms of the dot-product/cosine-similarity geometry used at inference time. The loss function's geometry must match the inference-time similarity metric — if you train with a loss that doesn't explicitly optimize cosine similarity (e.g., plain MSE reconstruction), there's no guarantee the resulting embedding space's cosine geometry is meaningful at all, which is precisely why the loss-function choice during embedding-model training directly determines whether downstream KNN/clustering (using cosine similarity) will actually work well.

**Q5. Your fraud-detection model needs a loss function reflecting that a missed fraud case costs 20x more than a false alarm. Walk through how you'd modify standard cross-entropy to reflect this, and any risks with your approach.**
Strong answer: Use weighted cross-entropy: multiply the loss for false negatives (missed positive/fraud cases) by a factor reflecting the cost ratio (e.g., weight the positive class's loss term by ~20x relative to the negative class's, tuned/validated rather than applied as a literal unadjusted ratio, since the optimal weight interacts with the base class imbalance too — see folder 21). Risk: over-aggressive weighting can push the model toward excessive false positives in a way that technically reduces expected cost on the training distribution but creates unacceptable operational friction (too many false alarms overwhelming a review team) — so the final weight/threshold combination should be validated against the *actual* deployed cost function and operational capacity constraints, not just the training loss in isolation, tying this back to the evaluation and threshold-tuning discussion in folder 20.

## Common Pitfalls & Follow-Up Probes

- Confusing the *training* loss function with the *evaluation* metric — they're often different by design (e.g., train with log-loss, evaluate with PR-AUC/F1) and a candidate should be able to explain why that's not inconsistent.
- Not knowing MAE's non-differentiability at zero requires subgradient methods or smoothed variants (Huber, pseudo-Huber) for gradient-based optimizers.
- Treating "loss function" as a fixed framework default rather than a deliberate choice tied to the error-cost structure of the actual business problem — this is the single biggest maturity signal across this entire topic.
